<Section label="Definition">
<Fragment slot="note">TMR is triple modular redundancy. Lyons and Vanderkulk described it in 1962, building on von Neumann (1956).</Fragment>

## The question

Fixed triple modular redundancy has a fixed threshold. Three processors vote. Two can still detect a disagreement. One cannot outvote anything, so the system stops.

On a mission with no repairs and no ground team in reach, that throws away the last processor's whole remaining life. The whitepaper proposes a shrinking quorum instead: vote while three are alive, compare while two are, self-check on one. Falling back from TMR to simplex is not new: the Space Shuttle's computers did it. The question is what it is worth when no ground team can command it, and how it compares with the autonomous designs spacecraft fly.

</Section>

<Section label="Parameters">
<Fragment slot="note">Shape 1.5 and a 125-year scale give a median processor life of about 98 years. The parameters are illustrative, not calibrated.</Fragment>

## The model

Each mission is a day-by-day fault-injection run. Processors die on a Weibull lifetime. Deaths can be correlated, as a shared thermal or power fault would be. Upsets corrupt a day's result at the rate that gets past EDAC and scrubbing.

Every policy runs against the same fault history and the same upsets. Any difference between policies is the policy.

</Section>

<Section label="Method">
<Fragment slot="note">Intervals are from a paired bootstrap: 2,000 resamples of whole missions.</Fragment>

## The cost

A lone self-checking processor misses a few of its upsets. Those become wrong results that nobody knew were bad. Fixed TMR never produces them, because it never lives long enough.

So the honest comparison is a price. Under the shrinking quorum, <Claim id="dusk.risk.shrink" /> of missions (95% CI <Claim id="dusk.risk.shrink" part="ci" />) deliver at least one wrong result. Under fixed TMR, <Claim id="dusk.risk.tmr" /> do. Fixed TMR comes out ahead only if one wrong result costs more than **<Claim id="dusk.break_even_years" /> years** of useful work (95% CI <Claim id="dusk.break_even_years" part="ci" />). Against one processor with spares (standby simplex), the shrinking quorum makes <Claim id="dusk.fewer_wrong_vs_simplex" /> fewer wrong results.

</Section>

**TMR with an unchecked fallback** steps down on its own but never checks its last processor. It does more useful work at every setting, with about 100 times the wrong results: the shrinking quorum makes <Claim id="dusk.wrong_vs_unchecked" /> of them (95% CI <Claim id="dusk.wrong_vs_unchecked" part="ci" />). That gap is what self-check buys.

**Cold standby** keeps one processor powered and switches on the next when it dies, the nearest model here of dual-string with autonomous switchover. An unpowered spare ages more slowly than a powered one, but nobody knows how much, so the rate is swept from 0.1 to 1.0. Below about <Claim id="dusk.cold.crossing" />, cold standby does more useful work than the shrinking quorum. Above it, the shrinking quorum does more. At every rate, cold standby has more missions with a wrong result, because it never votes.

## When the ground can help

Real spacecraft do not run TMR alone. When a string fails, a ground team commands a fallback by hand. Dusk adds that team as a baseline, for as long as support lasts.

## How sure are we

The sweep varies every parameter the model cannot pin down. Across every row, the shrinking quorum returned **<Claim id="dusk.sweep.range" />** the useful work of fixed TMR. The gain is largest with early, random failures and smallest with sharp wear-out, where all three processors die close together.

</Section>

<Section>

## What this does not claim

The parameters are plausible orders of magnitude, not values fitted to flight data. The claim is the shape of the trade, and the sweep shows where it holds. Power, thermal limits and duty cycling are not modeled. The ground team in the baseline is perfect, which flatters the ground. The aging rate of an unpowered spare is not measured. Correlated deaths in cold standby follow the powered fault history, which slightly under-counts them.

</Section>
